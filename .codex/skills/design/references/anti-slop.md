# Anti-slop review

These are default objections, not an absolute style prohibition. If an exception is necessary, name the product or domain reason in the design direction.

## Common reflexes

- Inter, Arial, Roboto, or a system stack used everywhere without a deliberate reason;
- purple-to-blue, pink-to-violet, or glowing mesh gradients used as generic atmosphere;
- one centered SaaS hero followed by an identical three-card feature grid;
- card-in-card nesting, oversized rounded containers, or decorative KPI/stat tiles without a decision they help users make;
- generic “trusted by”, testimonial, logo, or FAQ sections with no domain-specific content;
- pure black/white or gray-on-color text where a tinted neutral would improve hierarchy and contrast;
- emoji or mixed icon families in product controls;
- fake dashboards, fabricated metrics, placeholder people, or invented proof points;
- hover-revealed primary actions, hidden focus states, or buttons whose labels wrap awkwardly;
- page-load bounce, typewriter body copy, universal card lift, confetti on ordinary saves, or motion that delays the task.

## Category check

Before choosing a visual direction, ask what the category normally does and whether that reflex serves this product. Examples:

- AI is not automatically purple, black, and glowing.
- SaaS is not automatically blue, Inter, and three cards.
- Legal and fintech are not automatically navy, gold, and serif.
- Wellness is not automatically sage, cream, and script.

Keep trust, scanability, and task ergonomics ahead of novelty for operational software. Use stronger art direction when the subject, audience, and task justify it.

## Replacement rule

Notice the reflex, name it, and replace it with a choice tied to the subject. A visual decision should be defensible in one sentence and should improve hierarchy, orientation, comprehension, or product identity.
